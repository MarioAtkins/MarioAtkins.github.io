The Empty
Safe Deposit Box.

A safe deposit box is drilled. Years later, a customer claims seven million dollars in jewelry disappeared. The entire case turns on one notarized document.

A Fictional Notarial Ethics Case Study · Florida Statute 655.94 · No Verdict

Introduction

Read this first.

This is a dramatization, not a courtroom transcript. Real civil litigation does not unfold the way it does below. Witnesses are not recalled back-to-back in the same afternoon. Objections are not resolved in seconds. Discovery, depositions, and motion practice would ordinarily take months or years before any of this dialogue happened in front of a jury. The courtroom format was chosen to compress a discussion about notarial practice and institutional procedure into something listenable, not to depict how litigation actually proceeds.
Basic case posture, for readers who want the legal scaffolding:
  • Plaintiff: Samuel Barrett, the box lessee.
  • Defendant: Ocean View National Bank. Barrett is suing the institution, not any individual employee, since the notary and officer acted within the scope of their employment.
  • Alleged damages: $7,000,000, representing the value Barrett claims was in the box when it was drilled.
  • Underlying theory: the bank, through its officer and notary, failed to properly document and preserve the contents when the box was opened, which is why there is no reliable record of what it actually contained. A real version of this fact pattern would likely sound in negligence and/or breach of the institution's duty as bailee, though the dramatization doesn't litigate the cause of action by name.
  • What the bank disputes: that anything beyond bare statutory compliance was required, and that Barrett has offered no independent proof of what the box contained.
None of this is modeled on any actual lawsuit, reported case, or real institution. It is a hypothetical fact pattern built to dramatize a real structural question in Florida notarial practice. The exhibits, names, and institution below are fictional. Reasonable practitioners may disagree about the legal sufficiency of either approach shown in this case study, and that disagreement is the point.

PART ONE — OPENING STATEMENTS

The courtroom is tense as the jurors file in, their eyes scanning the room with a mix of curiosity and anticipation. The judge enters, and the buzz of whispers fades into silence. All rise. The gavel strikes sharply. The room goes silent as the judge takes their seat.

JUDGE: Please be seated. Today we have Barrett v. Ocean View National Bank. The plaintiff, Mr. Samuel Barrett, claims that the contents of his safe deposit box, including seven million dollars worth of jewelry, are missing following an unauthorized drilling by the bank. The defense asserts that all proper procedures were followed. At the center of this case is a single document: a notarized affidavit. Let's begin with opening statements.

Plaintiff's attorney, Miss Thompson, steps forward, holding the affidavit like it's the key to unraveling the entire case. She addresses the jury, her voice steady but sharp.

MISS THOMPSON: Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, this affidavit should have been the most reliable piece of evidence the bank could present. It's supposed to be sworn proof of what happened when the box was drilled open. But let's look closely at what actually took place. The notary who signed off on this document was not an impartial party. They were an employee of the bank, named directly in the affidavit, and they administered an oath to their own colleague — the bank officer who oversaw the drilling. This isn't just a minor issue. It's a fundamental problem that taints the entire process.

Miss Thompson steps closer to the jury, holding up the document for all to see.

MISS THOMPSON: This document lists three signatures: the locksmith who drilled the box, the bank officer who oversaw it, and the notary who notarized the affidavit. But here's the critical flaw. The locksmith's role ended the moment the box was drilled open. They didn't see the contents inside. They stepped out. So the only people left to verify what was inside were two bank employees: the officer, who is making a sworn statement, and the notary, who is supposed to be an impartial witness. But how can the notary be impartial when they are also named in the document and administering the oath to their own colleague?

The defense attorney, Mr. Davis, rises, appearing confident and unbothered.

MR. DAVIS: Members of the jury, this is a simple case of following procedure. Florida law doesn't require anything more than what was done here. A notary was present, an oath was administered, and the document was signed. Are we really to believe that because the notary works for the bank, they're incapable of performing their duties impartially? That's an absurd leap to make.

Miss Thompson fires back immediately, her voice filled with conviction.

MISS THOMPSON: Absurd? What's absurd is thinking that meeting the bare minimum legal standard is enough when you're dealing with millions of dollars in assets. This isn't a casual notarization for a basic document. This is about entering a safe deposit box, potentially containing anything from family heirlooms to priceless jewelry. And instead of using a certification, which would have simply attested to the presence of the notary without making them a party to the transaction, they chose a jurat. A jurat requires the notary to administer an oath and verify the truthfulness of the statement made. In doing so, they put the full weight of their own credibility behind a statement from a colleague. That's the risk.

The jury leans in, intrigued by the back and forth. Mr. Davis smirks slightly, turning back to address them.

MR. DAVIS: And what evidence do we have that seven million dollars worth of jewelry was even in this box? We're expected to take the plaintiff's word for it. There's no inventory list, no photos, nothing. They're making a claim without proof — and we're supposed to ignore the fact that the notary was simply doing their job as required by law. This is a textbook example of meeting all legal requirements.

Miss Thompson steps forward once more, unwavering, clearly ready for this counter.

MISS THOMPSON: Yes, Mr. Davis, the notary met the bare minimum requirements. But when you're dealing with assets valued at seven million dollars, is the bare minimum really enough? The bank officer made a sworn statement, and the notary — who was also an employee — vouched for it without documenting any details of what was actually removed from the box. There was no independent verification, no inventory taken, and no third party present to corroborate the contents. The bank chose convenience over diligence. And now we're left to deal with the consequences.

JUDGE: We will take a brief recess. This court will reconvene in fifteen minutes.

The courtroom buzzes with whispered conversations as the jury exits for recess, leaving the gallery to speculate.

— END OF PART ONE —

PART TWO — THE NOTARY TESTIFIES

The courtroom reconvenes after recess. The atmosphere is charged with anticipation as the judge strikes the gavel.

JUDGE: Court is back in session. Miss Thompson, you may call your first witness.

Miss Thompson rises with confidence, her expression focused.

MISS THOMPSON: Thank you, Your Honor. I call Miss Jessica Reed, the notary, to the stand.

Jessica Reed steps forward, visibly tense. She is sworn in and takes her seat, fidgeting slightly with her hands.

MISS THOMPSON: Miss Reed, you were the notary present during the drilling of the safe deposit box in question, on July 12th, three years ago. Is that correct?

JESSICA REED: Yes, that's correct.

Miss Thompson holds up a blown-up copy of the affidavit on a projector screen.

MISS THOMPSON: Let's take a look at this affidavit. This is the document you notarized, correct? And can you confirm that this is your signature at the bottom?

JESSICA REED: Yes, that's my signature.

Miss Thompson points to the section for contents.

MISS THOMPSON: Now let's focus on this section here, where the contents of the box should be listed. There's a diagonal strike-through. Did you make this mark, Miss Reed?

JESSICA REED: (visibly confused) I... I'm not sure. I mean, I don't recall making any adjustments outside of the notarial block.

MISS THOMPSON: You're not sure? As a notary, are you permitted to make any alterations or notations on a document outside of your designated notarial section?

JESSICA REED: No. We're only supposed to complete the notarial block.

MISS THOMPSON: (sensing an opportunity) Exactly. So if you didn't make this strike-through, then who did?

Jessica Reed's eyes widen as she realizes it must have been the bank officer.

JESSICA REED: I didn't make that mark.

MISS THOMPSON: (turning to the jury with a slight smile) So you're saying that the officer made this adjustment — the officer who also swore an oath about the contents of the box?

JESSICA REED: Yes, it seems so.

The courtroom stirs with murmurs. Mr. Davis quickly stands.

MR. DAVIS: Objection, Your Honor. The witness is speculating. There's no evidence that the officer made any unauthorized changes. This line of questioning is leading and assumes facts not in evidence.

JUDGE: Sustained. Miss Thompson, stick to what the witness can confirm directly.

MISS THOMPSON: Miss Reed, were you trained to verify or document the contents of a safe deposit box when it's drilled open?

JESSICA REED: No. We were only trained to witness the signing and complete the notarial block.

MISS THOMPSON: So you didn't note any specific items found in the box, nor did you keep a separate record in a notarial journal?

JESSICA REED: No, I didn't.

MISS THOMPSON: (firm, driving the point home) In other words, you have no independent record of what happened that day, aside from this affidavit — with a strike-through made by someone else.

JESSICA REED: No. No, I don't.

Miss Thompson turns back to the judge.

MISS THOMPSON: I have no further questions for this witness. I request to excuse Miss Reed for now, but I may need to recall her for further clarification.

JUDGE: Miss Reed, you are temporarily excused. You may step down.

Jessica Reed stands, visibly relieved, and exits the stand quickly. Miss Thompson remains composed as she turns to the judge once more.

MISS THOMPSON: Your Honor, I'd like to call the bank officer who was present during the drilling, Mr. Alan Peterson.

— END OF PART TWO —

PART THREE — THE OFFICER UNDER SCRUTINY

Alan Peterson steps up to the stand. He is visibly tense, his expression guarded, as he's sworn in and takes his seat.

MISS THOMPSON: Mr. Peterson, you were the officer in charge on the day the safe deposit box was drilled open. Is that correct?

ALAN PETERSON: Yes, that's correct.

MISS THOMPSON: Can you confirm that this is your signature on the affidavit?

ALAN PETERSON: Yes, that's my signature.

Miss Thompson points to the strike-through.

MISS THOMPSON: Did you make this diagonal strike-through in the contents section?

ALAN PETERSON: Yes, I did. It's a standard way to indicate that the box was empty.

MISS THOMPSON: Standard, you say. Did you consider writing "none found," or listing any items that might have been in the box, even if insignificant?

ALAN PETERSON: There was nothing of value to list. A simple strike-through was sufficient.

MISS THOMPSON: And you swore an oath to that effect, didn't you — that there was absolutely nothing in that box worth noting?

ALAN PETERSON: I... I did. Yes.

MISS THOMPSON: Mr. Peterson, this is a sworn statement. If you made this mark, you effectively documented that nothing was in the box. But can you confirm that you checked every item — every paper, every possible belonging inside — before making that decision?

ALAN PETERSON: It was three years ago. I don't remember every detail. I followed the procedure as we were trained.

MISS THOMPSON: (voice rising slightly) You don't remember, or you chose not to document it? Because there's a big difference, Mr. Peterson. You took an oath, and now we're left with a document that gives us nothing — no list, just a hasty strike-through.

Mr. Davis leaps to his feet.

MR. DAVIS: Objection, Your Honor. The plaintiff's counsel is badgering the witness. He has stated multiple times that he followed procedure. This is nothing more than a fishing expedition.

JUDGE: Sustained. Miss Thompson, wrap up your questioning.

MISS THOMPSON: (calm) Mr. Peterson, to the best of your recollection, did you ever confirm with the notary what was inside the box before finalizing this affidavit?

ALAN PETERSON: (tight-lipped) No, I didn't. It wasn't necessary.

MISS THOMPSON: No further questions, Your Honor. However, I request permission to briefly recall Miss Reed to the stand for clarification based on Mr. Peterson's testimony.

JUDGE: Permission granted.

— END OF PART THREE —

PART FOUR — THE GRILLING OF THE NOTARY

Jessica Reed returns to the stand, visibly more nervous now.

JUDGE: The witness is reminded that they are under oath. Miss Thompson, you may continue.

MISS THOMPSON: (calm, yet piercing) You were the notary named on this affidavit, and you administered the oath to your colleague. Correct?

JESSICA REED: Yes, that's correct.

MISS THOMPSON: And as we established, you did not keep a journal of this notarization. Is that also correct?

JESSICA REED: Yes, that's correct. The bank's policy doesn't require it.

MISS THOMPSON: This notarization took place nearly three years ago. Can you tell us what you remember about the contents of the safe deposit box that day?

JESSICA REED: (visibly nervous) I... I don't recall exactly.

MISS THOMPSON: You don't recall exactly? You were there when the box was opened. You administered the oath. You signed off on this affidavit. Yet you don't remember what was inside?

JESSICA REED: There are many of these procedures. I can't be expected to remember every single detail from years ago.

MISS THOMPSON: (smiling slightly, sensing an opportunity) That's exactly my point. Without a journal entry, there is no independent record of what took place. How can we trust the contents of this affidavit when even you — the notary — cannot recall the events clearly?

Mr. Davis rises.

MR. DAVIS: Objection, Your Honor. This is unfair. The notary followed the standard procedures outlined by the bank. This line of questioning is leading the witness into speculation.

JUDGE: Overruled. The witness will answer.

MISS THOMPSON: Let me ask you again. Do you remember any of the items that were listed as coming out of that box?

JESSICA REED: No, I don't remember the specifics. It was a routine procedure.

MISS THOMPSON: Routine? We're talking about a claim involving seven million dollars worth of jewelry. Is that your idea of a routine procedure?

JESSICA REED: I... I didn't know the value of the contents.

Miss Thompson stands tall, addressing the jury directly.

MISS THOMPSON: Ladies and gentlemen, this is the core issue. A notary who cannot recall the events, who didn't keep a journal, who administered an oath without verifying the contents — and yet we are expected to trust this process. This isn't just negligence. This is a failure of the entire procedure, one that jeopardizes the integrity of what a notary's role is supposed to protect.

Mr. Davis, visibly frustrated, stands to counter.

MR. DAVIS: The bank met every legal requirement. The notary acted according to policy. To place blame on her for not recalling every detail from three years ago is unreasonable. She followed the standard.

JUDGE: We'll take a brief recess before moving on to expert testimony.

The gavel strikes. The court is adjourned for a short recess.

— END OF PART FOUR —

PART FIVE — EXPERT TESTIMONY AND VERDICT

The notary, visibly rattled from her testimony, steps down from the witness stand. The defense attorney gives her a sympathetic nod, but it's clear she didn't deliver the solid defense they had hoped for. The judge rustles his papers, adjusting his glasses as he looks up.

JUDGE: Miss Thompson, please call your next witness.

MISS THOMPSON: (confident stride) Your Honor, the plaintiff calls Mr. Mark Clobert, expert in notary law.

The jury leans forward as Mark Clobert, a distinguished figure in his sixties with a calm demeanor, steps up. He is sworn in, takes the stand, and the room falls silent.

MISS THOMPSON: Mr. Clobert, you've been called here today to provide your expert analysis on the notarization process involved in this case. Can you begin by sharing your professional background?

MARK CLOBERT: Of course. I've spent over thirty years studying notarial law. I've consulted on hundreds of cases involving improper notarizations, and I am frequently called as an expert witness to evaluate the integrity of notarial acts. I'm also a published author on best practices for notaries, focusing on how to exceed minimum legal requirements.

MISS THOMPSON: Thank you. Now, based on your review of the affidavit in question, what are your main concerns with how the notarization was handled?

Mark Clobert leans slightly forward, addressing the jury.

MARK CLOBERT: The primary issue lies in the type of notarization chosen. A jurat was used, requiring an oath or affirmation from the officer. However, the notary was named directly in the document as a party, compromising her impartiality. Additionally, there was no record kept of the specific details of the contents removed from the box, and the notary admitted she could not recall whether she administered the oath properly. This points to serious deficiencies in both notarial practice and training.

Mr. Davis rises with a swift, almost predatory motion, ready to pounce.

MR. DAVIS: Mr. Clobert, let's get something straight. You're suggesting that every notary who doesn't keep a journal, or who follows their employer's protocols, is negligent?

MARK CLOBERT: (shaking his head slightly) Not at all. I'm saying that in high-risk situations like this, a prudent notary should take extra steps to ensure the integrity of the notarization. A journal is not legally required in Florida, but it is a best practice that helps protect the notary, the signers, and the institution. In this case, it would have provided crucial evidence of what transpired.

MR. DAVIS: (pressing harder) The notary followed the law, Mr. Clobert. The law does not mandate a journal, and it does not specify what kind of notarial act must be used in a safe deposit box drilling. Are you suggesting that your interpretation should override state law?

MARK CLOBERT: No, Mr. Davis. I am suggesting that the standard of care should be higher than just meeting the bare minimum legal requirements. The notary's role is to act as an impartial witness to the truth of the statements being made. In this case, that standard was compromised by the notary's involvement as a named party, and the choice of a jurat instead of a certification. A certification would have documented the facts of what was witnessed, reducing the risk to everyone involved.

The jury exchanges glances, sensing the gravity of Clobert's argument. Davis, however, is not ready to back down. He steps closer, his voice dripping with sarcasm.

MR. DAVIS: So you would have preferred a certification instead of a jurat? You believe that would have magically prevented this dispute over the seven million dollars in missing jewelry?

MARK CLOBERT: (unperturbed) It's not about preventing the dispute, Mr. Davis. It's about ensuring the integrity of the notarial act. A certification would have required the notary to observe and document the removal of the contents, providing an independent record. Instead, we have a flawed affidavit, relying solely on the officer's statement without sufficient corroboration. That's a recipe for exactly this kind of litigation.

Mr. Davis, almost shouting now, sensing the shift in the jury's sentiment.

MR. DAVIS: You're setting an impossible standard, Mr. Clobert. Are you really saying that every notary should question their employer's directives? That's absurd. Notaries aren't legal experts — they rely on their employers to set the procedures.

Mark Clobert raises a hand slightly, his voice stern and measured.

MARK CLOBERT: A notary's first duty is to the public, Mr. Davis. Not their employer. It is the notary's responsibility to understand the difference between a jurat and a certification, and to ensure that their actions uphold the integrity of the notarial act. If the employer's directive compromises that, the notary must speak up — or refuse to proceed. That's not an impossible standard. It's the very essence of the notary's role.

The courtroom falls silent. The defense attorney looks momentarily deflated, but he rallies for one final attempt.

MR. DAVIS: One last question, Mr. Clobert. You're saying the notary failed, the officer failed, and the employer failed. Isn't this just hindsight bias? The notary did what she was instructed to do, and now you're placing all the blame on her.

Mark Clobert nods slightly, his voice softening as he addresses the jury.

MARK CLOBERT: This isn't about hindsight, Mr. Davis. It's about foresight. A diligent notary anticipates these risks and takes steps to mitigate them. It's about understanding the weight of the seal, and the responsibility that comes with it. The notary's actions, the employer's training, and the choice of an affidavit all fell short of what's expected when dealing with something as sensitive as the contents of a safe deposit box. That's why we're here today.

The jury is visibly moved. The judge finally intervenes, sensing the exhaustion of both attorneys.

JUDGE: Thank you, Mr. Clobert. You may step down. We'll now proceed to closing arguments before this case is handed over to the jury.

As Clobert steps down, he makes brief eye contact with the notary, who looks down, visibly shaken. The judge bangs his gavel, and the courtroom settles once more. It's clear that Clobert's testimony has left a lasting impact. The air in the room feels heavy with anticipation as everyone braces for the final arguments — and the jury's decision.

The Questions.

  1. The statute does not expressly require either an affidavit or a sworn oath. Does the use of an affidavit add clarity, create unnecessary complexity, or alter the notary's role?
  2. Does naming the notary as an "observer" in the substantive body of a form change her legal posture under Florida Statute 117.107(12), even if she's still just an employee notarizing for her employer? Why or why not?
  3. Why might an institution prefer an officer's sworn affidavit over a notary's independent certificate, even though the certificate is the format the statute anticipates?

Part Four

Competing Perspectives.

The Institution

  • The affidavit exceeds statutory minimum requirements.
  • Multiple signatures increase accountability.
  • Employee-notaries routinely notarize for their employers.
  • No statute expressly prohibits this format.

The Critic

  • The statute contemplates a certificate rather than a sworn affidavit.
  • The form may blur the distinction between witness and certifying official.
  • The absence of an inventory increases evidentiary risk.
  • The form may create avoidable questions concerning impartiality.

The Practitioner

  • The process may be legally sufficient while still exposing the institution and notary to unnecessary litigation risk.
  • Best practices frequently exceed statutory minimums.
Part Five

Points of General Agreement.

  • Florida Statute 655.94 requires a notary to be present during certain safe deposit box openings and to execute a certificate regarding the contents.
  • The statute does not prescribe a specific certificate form.
  • Florida law generally permits employee-notaries to notarize documents for their employers, absent a prohibited interest.
  • Maintaining detailed contemporaneous records often reduces litigation risk.
  • Institutions may adopt procedures that exceed statutory minimum requirements.
Part Six

Governing Authorities.

Florida Statute 655.94

Governs the opening of safe deposit boxes for nonpayment of rent. Requires the box to be opened in the presence of an officer of the institution and a notary public. The notary public seals the contents in a package and executes a certificate reciting the lessee's name, the date of opening, and a list of the contents. The statute does not require an affidavit, an oath, or a jurat, and it does not prescribe a specific certificate form.

Florida Statute 117.107(12)

A notary public may not notarize a signature on a document if the notary has a financial interest in, or is a party to, the underlying transaction. An employee may notarize for their employer without that employment alone being disqualifying, so long as the notary receives no benefit beyond salary and the statutory notary fee.

National Notary Association Guidance

Because Florida law does not provide a form certificate for safe deposit box openings, the NNA's Florida Notary Primer publishes a suggested certificate format consistent with the statute's requirements, present, inventory, certify, seal, without an oath.
