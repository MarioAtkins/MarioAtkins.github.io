Atkins Fraud Analysis · Risk Operations There is a moment in fraud prevention that nobody talks about. It is not the moment the fraud happens. It is not the moment the member sits across from you, hands shaking, realizing their Social Security deposit is gone. The moment nobody talks about is the one that comes after all of that. When the member leaves. When the branch quiets. When you sit with what just happened and you think: this did not have to occur. I know how to fix it. · · · I spent fifteen years inside a financial institution. I managed fraud escalations, compliance workflows, branch operations, and the space between what the institution needed and what the member standing in front of me needed — which were not always the same thing. I learned early that fraudsters do not attack the institution. They attack the people. They attack the convenience the institution built for those people. Online banking. Remote access. Self-service transfers. All of it designed to reduce friction for the legitimate customer. All of it, simultaneously, a door left open for the person who has learned to perform as one. The dollar threshold is not identity verification. It is a speed bump. What the existing wire transfer authorization process cannot tell you is whether the person who logged in, filled out the form, and clicked submit is actually the person whose name is on the account. · · · Remote online notarization became legal in Florida on January 1, 2020. I know that date precisely because I was building a business around it. The process requires live appearance on camera, credential analysis, dynamic knowledge-based authentication, and a recorded session stored for ten years under law. Every commissioned notary carries a bond. Many carry errors and omissions insurance in addition. I mapped out what it would look like embedded into wire transfer authorization above a defined threshold. Not as friction for the member. As accountability for the fraudster. A recorded, identity-verified, legally weighted authorization event with a bonded recovery mechanism built in. I believed this was worth building. I still believe it. · · · Here is where I have to be honest with myself. I was right about the idea. I was not right about how I brought it forward. I went to the wrong rooms first. I sent communications dense with detail when they should have been simple invitations. I let my excitement outrun my understanding of the politics required to move it. I was an assistant branch manager. And in the architecture of a financial institution, that position does not determine the ceiling of what you can see. But it does determine whether anyone will look where you are pointing. I watched ideas die for all of those reasons. Including my own. · · · I am sharing this because I have looked into the faces of the people it costs. I have watched members learn that the money they counted on is gone. I have watched institutions do what the regulation permits, which is not always what the member believed they were owed. The wire fraud gap I identified still exists. The tool that addresses it still exists. The institutions still losing money to it still exist. I am no longer inside the institution. I no longer have to navigate the politics of advancing an idea through a hierarchy that was not built to receive it from where I was standing. So I am saying it here instead. Not as a complaint. As a pattern worth naming. The framework is in the carousel below. Freely given. The game is usually to be sold, not told. I have watched enough people lose enough money to be done playing that game.
