# The Idea That Never Made It Out of the Rock Pile

---

There is a moment in fraud prevention that nobody talks about.

It is not the moment the fraud happens. It is not the moment the member sits across from you, hands shaking, realizing their Social Security deposit is gone. It is not the moment you spend the next two hours locking down their account, changing their username, closing the compromised profile, opening a new one, switching direct deposits, filing the affidavit, notarizing it yourself because you are also a commissioned notary and the member does not have time to go anywhere else.

The moment nobody talks about is the one that comes after all of that.

When the member leaves. When the branch quiets. When you sit with what just happened and you think: this did not have to occur. And you think: I know exactly where the gap is. And then you think the most dangerous thought of all.

I know how to fix it.

---

I spent fifteen years inside a financial institution. I started at the front line, helping members with the full range of what a credit union offers. Loans. Deposits. Service recovery. The daily texture of people's financial lives. Over time I moved into leadership. I became an assistant branch manager. I managed people, managed audits, managed cash operations, managed fraud escalations, managed the space between what the institution needed and what the member standing in front of me needed, which were not always the same thing.

And I became, without anyone asking me to, something of an informal expert in fraud.

Not because I sought the title. Because the fraud kept coming, and I was there, and I stayed.

I learned early that fraudsters do not attack the institution. They attack the people. They attack the convenience the institution built for those people. Online banking. Remote access. Self-service transfers. All of it designed to reduce friction for the legitimate customer. All of it, simultaneously, a door left open for the person who is not the legitimate customer but has learned to perform as one.

I learned that the debit card fraud that hits a member's account on a Tuesday is not the same legal or regulatory animal as the wire transfer that drains their savings on a Wednesday. The recoupment paths are different. The liability standards are different. The institutional posture toward each is different. I learned Regulation E. I learned what it protected and what it did not. I learned that when a member hands over their access device, even under duress, even under manipulation, the institution's obligation shifts in ways most members never understand until the moment they need to understand it most.

I watched people lose money they could not afford to lose. I watched the institution do what it was legally permitted to do, which was not always what the member believed it was obligated to do.

And through all of it, I kept coming back to one place in the process where I believed the exposure was largest and the protection was thinnest.

Wire transfers.

---

Here is what the authorization process for a wire transfer looked like from where I stood.

A member submits a request. Online, typically. They fill out the form. They provide the beneficiary information. Routing number. Account number. Purpose. They upload a copy of their identification. They click submit. The request moves to a queue. A specialist in member services or the payments department receives it, reviews it, confirms the details, runs the required compliance checks, and if everything passes, executes the wire.

There is a dollar threshold built into that process. A limit designed to create friction for large transactions. To slow things down enough that someone can review them.

That limit is not identity verification. It is a speed bump.

Because here is what that process cannot tell you. It cannot tell you whether the person who logged into that online banking account, navigated to the wire transfer form, filled it out correctly, uploaded an identification document, and clicked submit is actually the person whose name is on the account.

It can tell you that someone knew the username. Someone knew the password. Someone completed whatever multifactor authentication the platform required, whether that was a security question, a text message code, or a one-time PIN. It can tell you that someone completed the form correctly and uploaded something that looks like a driver's license.

It cannot tell you that the person is who they say they are.

And I knew, from the fraud cases I worked, that this gap was not theoretical. It was a recurring operational reality. People were losing money through wire transfers they did not authorize. The institution was reviewing what existed in the process and, in many cases, finding that the process had been followed correctly. That the fraud had been executed through legitimate channels. That the liability, by the standards that governed it, was difficult to place.

Members were being told, in various forms, that the money was gone and the options were limited.

I sat with that for a long time.

---

The solution I kept arriving at was not new technology. It was existing technology applied to a new context.

Remote online notarization became legal in Florida on January 1, 2020. I know that date precisely because I was building a business around it at the time. During the period when the world went indoors and the branch where I worked temporarily closed its doors to walk-in traffic and people still needed documents signed and notarized and certified, I became a remote online notary. I worked the platform. I learned it thoroughly. I notarized mortgages and reverse mortgages and home equity lines and general notary work and power of attorney documents and everything in between, appearing in living rooms and offices and makeshift home workspaces across multiple states, conducting identity verification sessions that were recorded from start to finish, credential-analyzed, journalized, and stored for a minimum of ten years under Florida law.

I learned what the process actually required.

The person had to appear on camera. They had to present a government-issued identification document. The platform performed credential analysis on that document, examining it against known markers of authenticity. The notary examined the document and the person simultaneously, comparing the face on the credential to the face on the screen. Dynamic knowledge-based authentication questions were asked. Questions drawn from public records that were specific enough that the person would know the answers but difficult enough that someone researching them would face a meaningful obstacle. The session was recorded. A journal entry was created. A copy of the completed and notarized document was transmitted to all relevant parties.

When the session was done, there was a record. A real one. Not a log of keystrokes and IP addresses. A recording of a human being, showing their face, presenting their identification, answering questions, swearing to the contents of what they were signing.

I understood intuitively and then increasingly specifically that this process, embedded into wire transfer authorization, would change the evidentiary position of the institution and the member in a way that the existing process could not.

Here is what I mapped out.

When a member initiates a wire transfer above a certain threshold, the process does not immediately route to queue review. Instead, it routes to a remote online notarization session. The member is notified. They schedule, or in a twenty-four-hour platform model, they initiate on demand. They appear before a commissioned notary. They are identified. They are recorded. They sign the authorization document not with a click but with a sworn acknowledgment. The notary journals the transaction. The recording is stored.

The institution then receives not just a form submission but a notarized, recorded, identity-verified authorization event. With a bonded recovery mechanism built in. Because every commissioned remote online notary working on a professional platform carries a bond. In Florida, the minimum for remote online notarization work is twenty-five thousand dollars. Many carry more. Many carry errors and omissions insurance in addition to the bond. The platform itself may carry additional coverage.

If fraud occurs despite that process, and someone has deceived a notary, there is now a recording of that deception. There is a credential that was presented. There is a face on camera. There is a sworn statement. There are avenues to accountability that the previous process did not create. And there is a financial recovery path through the notary's bond and insurance that did not exist before.

I believed this was worth building. I still believe it.

---

Here is where I have to be honest with myself.

I was right about the idea. I was not right about how I brought it forward.

I went to the wrong rooms first. I reached out to people at levels I had not yet built the trust to approach. I sent communications that were dense with detail when they should have been simple invitations to a conversation. I let my excitement about the solution outrun my understanding of the politics required to move it.

I submitted proposals through channels that were new and not yet fully trusted. I spent hours on spreadsheets and workflow diagrams and ROI projections that no one had asked me to produce and that, when presented without the relationship to give them credibility, read as the ambitious overreach of someone operating outside their lane.

I was an assistant branch manager. And in the architecture of a financial institution, an assistant branch manager who builds a proposal touching payment services, fraud operations, member services, compliance, vendor relationships, and legal liability is not typically received as a systems thinker. They are typically received as someone who does not understand their role.

I understood my role. I also understood that my role did not determine the ceiling of what I could see.

That tension. Between what I saw and what I was permitted to say about what I saw. Between the idea and the channel required to advance it. Between the value I believed I was offering and the credibility the institution was willing to extend. That tension was real, and I did not always navigate it well, and I carry my portion of that honestly.

But I also know that an idea does not fail only because of the person who carries it. Sometimes it fails because the organization is not yet in enough pain to be willing to change. Sometimes the people in the rooms that matter are measured on metrics that make friction look like a problem rather than a feature. Sometimes the political cost of accepting an idea from the wrong person outweighs the operational benefit of the idea itself.

I watched ideas die for all of those reasons. Including my own.

---

There is a version of this story where I compare myself to the engineer at Kodak who saw digital coming and could not get the institution to listen. I have thought about that comparison. I have resisted it, mostly because I am aware of how self-serving it can sound. The person who was not heard always believes their idea was worth hearing. That is not always true.

But I will say this.

The wire fraud gap I identified still exists. Remote online notarization is still underutilized as an authorization mechanism in financial services. Institutions are still losing money to transfer fraud that leaves limited recovery paths. The tool that addresses it is still legal, still operational, still supported by professional platforms that have been refining the process for years.

I am no longer inside the institution. I no longer have to navigate the politics of advancing an idea through a hierarchy that was not built to receive it from where I was standing.

So I am saying it here instead.

Not as a complaint. Not as a product pitch. Not as a resume item dressed up as thought leadership.

As a pattern worth naming.

Because the experience of watching a good idea die in the wrong room is not unique to me. Every organization has people in it who see something clearly and cannot get the institution to look where they are pointing. The cost of that gap. The gap between what someone sees and what the organization is willing to hear. is not always visible on a balance sheet. But it is real. And it accumulates.

If you work in fraud risk, compliance, or financial operations and you have watched this happen from either side of the table. I would like to hear about it.

And if you want to see the framework, I will share it.

---

*I turn unresolved complexity into defensible action.*

*That is not a tagline. It is what I have been doing, in one form or another, for fifteen years.*

*It just took January 27, 2026 to finally give it a name.*
